BiiiiG Stretch is a shoe stretcher that someone with low hand strength can use on their own. I designed it as the Design Fellow on a four-person team in the Open Style Lab Accessible Footwear Kits fellowship (summer 2025, New Lab, Brooklyn). The team won the Community Choice Award for Most Impactful Project.

## The problem

Our disability expert fellow has a small foot with a high volume: the length of a kids' shoe, the width and height of an adult foot. Kids' shoes are too tight and don't match her style, and adult shoes in her size barely exist. In one store, about 170 adult shoes were too big and about 60 kids' shoes were too tight.

When a shoe she loved didn't fit, someone else had to stretch it by hand. She told us she didn't want to depend on her brother as a "human shoe stretcher." Her feet also swell over the day, so a shoe that fits in the morning can pinch by evening.

## Research

We started with an interview using the PEO model (Person, Environment, Occupation), then mapped her day from dressing to a kitchen shift to evening classes, and marked where swelling changed what she could do. That became a design requirements board with four main needs:

- A shoe that can be stretched
- Pressure relief in specific tight spots
- Easy grip that needs little strength
- Independent use

OSL's broader accessible-footwear research told us this wasn't only her problem. Stretch, support and independence are the things people say are hardest to find in shoes. That research was done by OSL before our team started, and I'm citing it as context, not as my own work.

## Gaps in what's on the market

Existing stretchers mostly widen or lengthen a shoe. They don't lift the top of the foot, which is where swelling hurts. They also take high torque and fine finger grip to turn, and most are too big for small shoes.

That gave us three goals: add vertical lift, make the controls easy to turn, and make the whole thing modular and small-shoe friendly.

## The design

![Annotated sketch of the stretcher's main features](/images/osl/osl-features.jpg)

We kept the part of a traditional stretcher that works and rebuilt the parts that don't.

- **Targeted plugs.** Interchangeable plugs sit over the bunion, the bridge and the metatarsal area. Different plug heights give vertical lift for swelling.
- **A body sized for small shoes.** We modeled it in CAD from the stretcher she already found most comfortable, so it follows the natural curve of the shoe and stretches evenly.
- **A turning mechanism for low strength.** A flexible drill extender lets you turn the screw from an angle and from further away. You can also turn the knob directly.
- **Large textured knobs.** We tested several sizes and shapes with her to find ones her fingers could hold.

## What we found

We left the stretcher in her shoe for 48 hours and compared it to an untreated shoe. She felt less pressure over the bunion and the bridge, and said it was easier to use and kept the look of her shoe. We also asked her to guess which shoe had been stretched, and she picked the right one.

In her words, the dips in the knobs help her fingers stay in place, and she can turn it with her hands closed.

## Limits

This was one user, one pair of shoes and one 48-hour trial, so it shows the concept works for her but isn't evidence it works for everyone. The next steps would be testing with more people with different hand abilities and foot shapes, and more shoe types.
